VetMed Test Matrix

Comparing 2 Distinct Groups

Independent Samples t-test

1 Nominal IV (2 Levels) 1 Continuous DV

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Comparing the mean recovery time (in hours) between dogs that received standard surgery versus laser surgery.
  • Comparing the means of two unrelated, separate groups (e.g., Male vs. Female, Treatment vs. Control).
  • Dependent variable is Continuous (Interval/Ratio).

DO NOT USE WHEN:

  • You have 3 or more surgical protocols (Use ANOVA).
  • The dependent variable is Ordinal (e.g., Lameness Score 1-5).

Paired Samples t-test

1 Nominal IV (Time/Condition) 1 Continuous DV

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Comparing a canine patient's systolic blood pressure exactly 1 hour before and 1 hour after administering a hypertensive drug.
  • Comparing means from the exactly same subjects measured twice.

DO NOT USE WHEN:

  • The two groups consist of completely different animals.

Comparing 3 or More Groups

One-Way ANOVA

1 Nominal IV (3+ Levels) 1 Continuous DV

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Comparing the mean weight gain (in kg) of piglets fed three different commercial feeds (Diet A vs. Diet B vs. Diet C).
  • Data is normally distributed within each group.
  • When you need Tukey Post-Hoc tests to find out exactly *which* diet was superior.

DO NOT USE WHEN:

  • The dependent variable is an Ordinal rank (Use Kruskal-Wallis).
  • You have two independent variables (e.g., Diet AND Breed) — Use Two-Way ANOVA instead.

Analyzing Frequencies & Proportions

Chi-Square Test of Independence

2 Categorical Variables (Nominal/Ordinal)

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Determining if there is an association between the retail source (Wet Market vs. Supermarket) and the presence of Salmonella (Positive vs. Negative) in raw chicken meat.
  • Both variables are just groups or categories, no continuous measurements are taken.

DO NOT USE WHEN:

  • You have a continuous dependent variable (e.g., Bacterial count in CFU/g).

Chi-Square Goodness of Fit

1 Categorical Variable

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Comparing the *observed* frequency of Feline Lower Urinary Tract Disease (FLUTD) cases across four seasons to see if they deviate from an *expected* equal distribution (25% per season).

DO NOT USE WHEN:

  • You are testing for an association between *two* different variables (like Season AND Neuter Status).

Non-Parametric Alternatives (Data Violations)

Mann-Whitney U Test

Alternative to Independent t-test

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Comparing the Body Condition Score (1-9 ordinal scale) of adult dogs fed a homemade diet versus those fed a commercial diet.
  • The continuous dependent variable heavily violates the Shapiro-Wilk normality assumption.

Kruskal-Wallis H Test

Alternative to One-Way ANOVA

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Comparing Lameness Scores (0 to 5) in horses treated with three different analgesic medications (Meloxicam, Carprofen, Placebo).
  • Comparing 3 or more independent groups with ordinal or severely skewed data.

Data Visualization Selection

Bar Charts (Count Plots)

Best for Categorical Data

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Visualizing the total count of positive vs. negative Parvovirus cases observed at three different clinic locations.
  • Visually representing data from Chi-Square tests.

DO NOT USE WHEN:

  • Attempting to show the distribution spread or skewness of a single continuous variable (Use a Histogram).

Histograms

Best for Single Continuous Variables

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Assessing the bell-curve distribution and skewness of hemoglobin levels (g/dL) across a population of neonatal piglets.
  • Checking the normality assumption visually prior to a t-test or ANOVA.

DO NOT USE WHEN:

  • Comparing differences between multiple distinct groups (Use a Box Plot).

Box Plots (Box and Whisker)

Best for Group Comparisons

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Comparing the spread of recovery times (hours) across three different abdominal surgical techniques, immediately revealing medians and outliers.
  • Perfect companion visual for Independent t-tests, ANOVAs, and their non-parametric equivalents.

Scatterplots

Best for Continuous vs. Continuous

WHEN TO USE:

  • VetMed Example: Plotting a dog's age (in years) against its liver enzyme levels (ALT in U/L) to observe if levels linearly increase as the dog gets older.
  • Visually checking for linear relationships prior to running Pearson's Correlation or Linear Regression.
